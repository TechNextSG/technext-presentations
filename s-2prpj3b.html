Illustrative composite · sample data

Weftwood is a sample, but the shape is common: eight stores, one warehouse, two seasonal collections, around 240 styles a season. Click the multipliers, or let them build. One linen shirt is not one product: three colours times five sizes is fifteen variants, and each variant lives in nine places, eight stores plus the warehouse. That is 135 stock numbers for one shirt, and 32,400 for the season. The tills only know what they sold, the stock sheet is a week old, and the loyalty app knows the customer but not the basket. Nothing here is broken. It just can’t be seen at the size-and-store level, which is exactly where apparel retail is won or lost.

Sample figures

This is the knot. Press By size and store. The weekly report gives the buyer one number per style: 190 of 320 shirts sold by week 6, 59 percent, on plan. Nothing to do. Now open the same shirt by size and store. Size M is sold out in Tampines, Bugis and Punggol, in Tampines since week 3, while Novena and Holland Village are sitting on 17 size M shirts between them. The customer who wanted an M in Tampines walked out. The stock that would have served her is twenty minutes away on the MRT. Nobody is careless: the data that shows it simply doesn’t exist in one place.

Sample figures · one style, one colour

Drag the season, or press the arrow to jump to week 12. This is why it is structurally hard, not just a bad week. Size M sells out early in three stores, so the customers who wanted it are lost: in this sample 44 of them, about S$2,552 of margin at S$58 a shirt. Meanwhile the slow sizes and the stranded M get marked down: 30 percent in week 8, 50 percent in week 11, and another S$3,978 of margin is given away. Then the cruel part: at the end of the season all 320 shirts have sold, so the report says the size curve was right and the buyer orders the same curve again. Sold-out sizes record no demand, so the till can never show what was wanted. That is S$6,530 of margin on one style in one colour, and Weftwood has 240 styles. The answer is a loop, because a retail season is a loop: buy, receive, allocate, sell, reward, rebalance, mark down, read the season, and buy again. Every step is a standard Odoo 20 app on one database. Click each step. What changes is not any single screen: it is that the product, with its sizes and colours as variants, is the same record from the purchase order to the till to the dashboard, so the size-and-store view from slide 3 is a report, not a project. Loyalty is part of Point of Sale through Discount and Loyalty programmes; eCommerce can join the same loop later on the same stock.

Illustrative · sample data

Press the arrow, or click the steps. One: the dashboard shows size M by store, and a rule we agree with Weftwood’s merchandiser proposes the moves: take M from stores holding more than eight weeks of cover and send it to the stores that are out. Honest note: that proposal is something we set up for Weftwood, as a dashboard view or a small automation, not a standard Odoo button, and the merchandiser approves it. Two: each move becomes a standard transfer between warehouses, a delivery out of Novena and a receipt into Tampines, with the stock in an inter-warehouse transit location while it is on the van. Three: Tampines scans the shirts in with the Barcode app, and they are on sale at full price, not on the 30 percent rail in week 8.

Follow one customer with the arrow. In Tampines the cashier sets the customer on the order, and the size M is there because of the transfer on the last slide. The order earns 159 points on a loyalty card: a Discount and Loyalty programme of the Loyalty Cards type, available in Point of Sale; leaving its Point of Sale field empty makes it work at every till. Two weeks later in Novena the same card shows 412 points, and the Rewards button takes S$20 off. The customer’s contact shows a Loyalty Cards smart button and every order from every store. The points rule and the reward are Weftwood’s choice: these are sample values. Moving members from the old app means importing their balances, which we plan in phase 3.
